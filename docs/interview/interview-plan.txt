Yes. Given your **~10 years of experience**, current Senior SWE role, and the possibility that you may need an H-1B transfer quickly after a layoff, I would optimize almost entirely for **passing Senior Software Engineer interviews**, not for becoming encyclopedic.

Your preparation should have **4 tracks only**:

| Track | Time | Goal |
|---|---:|---|
| Coding / LeetCode | **40%** | Clear coding rounds |
| System Design | **30%** | Clear senior-level design rounds |
| Your experience + behavioral | **20%** | Demonstrate 10-year/senior-level impact |
| Job-specific tech | **10%** | Java/Python/AWS/Kafka/Kubernetes etc. |

### 1. Coding: target ~120–150 problems

Don't randomly click LeetCode problems. Learn **interview patterns**.

Focus heavily on:

- Arrays/strings: HashMap, two pointers, sliding window, prefix sum, intervals
- Trees: DFS, BFS, BST, recursion, LCA
- Graphs: BFS/DFS, topological sort, union-find, shortest path
- DP: 1-D DP, 2-D DP, knapsack-style, subsequences
- Binary search
- Heap / priority queue
- Stack/queue
- Linked lists

Skip obscure algorithms unless a target company's interview history suggests otherwise.

Your success criterion isn't "I completed 150." It is:

> Give me an unseen Medium → I identify the pattern → explain brute force → improve it → code it correctly → discuss complexity, in **30–35 minutes**.

That's interview readiness.

### 2. System design: extremely important for you

At ~10 YOE, I'd put **more emphasis here than I would for someone with 3–5 years experience**.

Don't spend weeks reading distributed-systems textbooks. Learn enough theory to **design systems in an interview**.

First learn these concepts:

**Load balancer → API gateway → caching → SQL vs NoSQL → indexing → replication → partitioning/sharding → queues/Kafka → CDN → object storage → consistency → rate limiting → idempotency → retries/DLQ → monitoring.**

Then immediately start designing.

You should be able to handle roughly **15–20 common designs**, such as:

> URL shortener
> Rate limiter
> Notification system
> Chat/messaging system
> News feed
> File storage/Dropbox
> YouTube/video streaming
> Uber/location service
> Ticket booking
> Payment system
> Search/autocomplete
> Metrics/logging system
> Distributed job scheduler
> E-commerce/order system
> Kafka/event-processing pipeline

For every problem, follow essentially the same interview structure:

**Requirements → scale estimates → APIs → data model → high-level architecture → deep dive → bottlenecks/failures → trade-offs.**

You don't need to memorize architectures. You need that process to become automatic.

### 3. Your 10 YOE is actually another interview subject

This is the part I think you should **not underestimate**.

A senior interviewer can forgive forgetting some Kubernetes command. They will have a much harder time overlooking someone with 10 YOE who cannot clearly explain:

**"Tell me about the most complex system you've built."**

You already have useful material from your Nike work: ingestion/data pipelines, AWS, Lambda, S3, RDS, Kafka, monitoring, CI/CD, migrations, etc.

Turn your experience into about **8 polished stories**:

1. Most technically challenging project
2. Architecture/design decision you made
3. Major production incident
4. Performance/scalability improvement
5. Disagreement with another engineer
6. Ambiguous project you drove
7. Failure/mistake and what changed afterward
8. Mentoring/leadership/cross-team influence

For each, know the **scale, architecture, your exact contribution, trade-offs, measurable result and what you'd change today**.

This is where your 10 years should become an advantage rather than merely a number on the résumé.

### 4. Don't study technologies randomly

This is where I think you can save a lot of time.

Don't decide:

> "Today I'll learn Kubernetes."
> "Tomorrow I'll study Kafka."
> "Maybe I should learn Terraform."
> "AI is popular, maybe I'll learn agents."

That's exactly the diversion you're trying to avoid.

Instead, study technology **through interview questions**.

For Kafka, for example:

**Why Kafka? → partition → consumer group → ordering → offset → delivery semantics → duplicate processing → rebalancing → DLQ → Kafka vs SQS → scaling consumers.**

If you can answer those comfortably and discuss how you've used Kafka, **stop**. Don't take a 30-hour Kafka course.

Same for Kubernetes:

**Pod → Deployment → ReplicaSet → Service → ingress → ConfigMap/Secret → readiness/liveness → requests/limits → autoscaling → rolling deployment → debugging.**

Then stop unless your target role specifically demands deeper Kubernetes knowledge.

### Your October → January roadmap

You have roughly three months, so I'd structure it like this.

**October — Foundation**

Coding: ~50 problems, especially arrays, trees and graphs.

System design: learn the core building blocks and complete ~5 designs.

Experience: prepare your résumé and your first 4 major project stories.

**November — Interview level**

Coding: another ~50 problems, introducing more graph/DP/heap questions.

System design: ~8 more designs.

Start doing **timed coding** instead of leisurely solving.

Prepare all behavioral stories.

And **start applying**. Don't wait until you're "ready."

**December — Simulation**

Another ~30–50 coding problems, but now mostly weaknesses and company-specific questions.

Do complete **45-minute coding interviews**.

Do complete **45–60-minute system-design interviews**.

Practice explaining your Nike/U.S. Bank projects without preparation.

Applications/referrals should be running heavily by this point.

**January — interview pipeline, not studying**

Ideally, January isn't:

> "I finally finished my preparation. Time to apply."

It should be:

> "I already have several interview processes running."

That difference is particularly important with H-1B because you don't want your job search starting only after your employment ends.

### Your daily routine

If you can manage **3 hours/day**, I'd do:

**90 min LeetCode → 60 min System Design → 30 min behavioral/project/tech questions.**

On weekends, replace one session with a mock interview.

There is one rule I'd use to decide whether something deserves your study time:

> **"Is there a realistic chance an interviewer will ask me this?"**

If **yes**, learn it. If **no**, bookmark it for later.

That means for the next three months I'd put **AI side projects, deep Kubernetes study, certifications, random courses, obscure LeetCode algorithms, and general computer-science reading** behind interview preparation.

For your situation, I would rather see you enter January with **130 LeetCode problems mastered + 15 system designs + 8 excellent experience stories + active interviews** than with 10 new technologies on your résumé.
